Thoughts about painting, art, and artist statements.

The older I get, my ideas around painting and Art change. Painting or any other art form is an extension of verbal language. I was talking to my friend last night and we talked about Richard Tuttle's work. And how his paintings start with words and their meaning create the forms. It made me think of many different strategies and ideas around the creative process and how they are tied to language. It reminded me how many people including myself write bad artist statements. I think in the development of work its good to look at the overall patterns in a portfolio. How are structure, materials, composition, and color linked? What is the relationship between the audience and those relationships?

My Ideas on How to Write an Artist Statement

Nobody ever gives truly good advice on how to write an artist statement. The best advice I got was anyone made mention of was, "Your work should sound how it feels". Which was little unclear but got me thinking about how most people including myself. Are either poor writers or never learn how to write a decent statement. My thoughts are this, a good statement must introduce your idea in a brief opening line. Then transition into "I explore X through paintings". In other words, talk about what theme, concept, or the general idea you explore through formal decisions. Then discuss why you are interested in that theme/idea, influences, and how it shapes your process. Then describe the work's size and then conclude it. The tone of your writing must match the tone of your work. Lastly who is your audience? Essentially it comes to four bullet points:

  • Why and how?
  • Tone
  • Size of work
  • Audience?

Check out White Columns Artist Registry to see a mix of good and bad statements.

Understanding Color

As a painter, color is incredibly important. It has the ability to convey mood, space, time, emotions, and much more. Color is deceptive because it appears to be simple, because it can be. But to use it effectively is the challenge. I feel like everyone should take a deep dive into it and study Josef Albers. I think a lot of painters/artists overlook color. It's hard to apply complex color theory. Ideas around the Bezold effect or transparency are difficult to use intentionally to convey an idea.

The Importance of Drawing

Learning how to develop a drawing routine is essential I think for any studio life. I am biased, but learning observational drawing and figure drawing is imperative if you want to understand pictorial space. It makes you a more intelligent artist and it gives you a sense of how art has changed over time. Personally, I think rendering is boring. Shapes are more interesting. It also shows what you do not know and helps you get used to making bad work. Failure is the best teacher.

Make Bad Work

I personally believe if you make enough work, you know what is good and bad. Making terrible work shows what you don't understand and helps you listen to others. Failures and experiments can show you where your work is headed. A great example was when I wanted to be a figurative painter, but all my figures were lazy, disengaged, and I realized shapes and color were more important. Sometimes it takes a while.

Learning How to Listen

I believe you should always listen to feedback. You will learn what good feedback is and what bad feedback is. When you first get into art making, people will say all sorts of things. Sometimes it's good and sometimes bad. If you're in school, always listen to the faculty. But stop listening when the feedback is taking over the work or when you realize some advice is not helpful. The same goes for peers or random people. If someone is rude to you, ignore them.

Make Art to Entertain Yourself if You're Bored

I personally view painting as a game. It's like a puzzle. Thinking about it helps me through awful work days and horrible life experiences, and gives me a reason to enjoy life. I am lucky in that way. Sometimes people want me to make funny work again. But I don't think I want to, mostly because I am not sure if it would help my work. I like funny art. But I also like work that feels like it is an extension of a lived experience. So thinking of it like a game helps keep me grounded.
